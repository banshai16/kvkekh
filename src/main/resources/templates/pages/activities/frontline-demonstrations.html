What is a frontline demonstration?

The frontline demonstration is a concept of field demonstration evolved by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research with the inception of the Technology Mission on Oilseed Crops in the mid-1980s. Field demonstrations conducted under the close supervision of scientists of the National Agricultural Research System are called frontline demonstrations because the technologies are demonstrated for the first time by the scientists themselves, before being fed into the main extension system of the State Department of Agriculture.

The main objective of frontline demonstrations is to demonstrate newly released crop production and protection technologies and their management practices on farmers' fields under different agro-climatic regions and farming situations. While demonstrating the technologies, scientists study the factors contributing to higher crop production and the field constraints of production, and thereby generate production data and feedback information. Frontline demonstrations are conducted in blocks of two to four hectares, to have a better impact on farmers and field-level extension functionaries.

Special features

Frontline demonstrations differ from the normal demonstrations conducted by extension functionaries:

  • They are conducted under the close supervision of scientists of the National Agricultural Research System, comprising ICAR institutes, National Research Centres, Project Directorates, Krishi Vigyan Kendras, and the State Agricultural Universities and their regional research stations.
  • Only newly released technologies, or those likely to be released in the near future, are selected.
  • They are organised in a block of two to four hectares, involving all farmers whose plots fall in the identified demonstration block.
  • Only critical inputs and training are provided from the scheme budget; the remaining inputs are supplied by the farmers themselves.
  • Training of the farmers associated with the demonstration is a prerequisite.
  • The target audience is both farmers and extension officers. The purpose is to convince extension functionaries and farmers together of the potential of the technologies for wider diffusion.

Frontline demonstrations are also used to generate data on the factors contributing to higher crop yields and the constraints of production under various farming situations.

Field demonstrations

A field demonstration is a long-term educational activity conducted systematically on farmers' fields to show the worth of a new practice or technology. "Seeing is believing" is its basic philosophy, so only proven technologies are selected. Field demonstrations educate farmers through the results obtained — varieties resistant to disease and pests, grain quality and higher yields — and in terms of input-output ratio and net returns. There are two types:

Single practice demonstration
Proves the worth of a single practice, such as the effect of balanced fertilisers in rice, higher yields from newly released hybrid maize varieties, irrigation at the crown root initiation stage of wheat, or a new pesticide against fruit borer in gram.
Composite demonstration
Combines field-based result demonstrations with a chain of skill-oriented method demonstrations, to demonstrate the superiority of a package of practices for a field crop. The effect of one practice in harnessing the effect of others is also demonstrated — for example, the combined effect of irrigation and fertiliser application on the yield and quality of a newly released mustard variety.

Good field demonstrations lead to higher adoption of the demonstrated practices because they build farmers' confidence. Farmers see the crops themselves, interact with scientists and extension workers in the field, and get their doubts clarified on the spot. Demonstrations must therefore be well planned and executed: one bad demonstration can undo the impact of many good ones. Demonstrations should be organised only with a specific purpose and when the situation demands.

Steps in conducting a field demonstration

A. Planning phase

Know the vicinity
Understand the farmers, their farming systems and resources, and establish rapport with them. Gather information on cropping systems, present use of inputs and the productivity of major crops — by visiting villages and farmers, using PRA tools, meeting people individually and in groups, meeting opinion leaders, exchanging information with local extension workers, and consulting office records of population and agriculture.
Select technologies
Select only proven, recently released (or soon to be released) technologies with higher potential in yield, disease resistance and quality, which fit the existing farming systems of the area. Be sure the technology is clearly superior to the one already in use, in consultation with the research scientists responsible for its release.
Select the demonstration site
Avoid isolated farms. The site should be easily accessible to farmers and extension workers and, as far as possible, the block should include farmers of all categories of land holding. Never conduct a block demonstration on a single farmer's plot. Consider farm size, field layout, soil type, fertility, irrigation and drainage.
Select demonstration farmers
Select farmers with land in the demonstration block who are willing to co-operate, finally at a village meeting where the purpose is clearly stated and suggestions are sought. Resolve differences of opinion tactfully, to avoid non-co-operation from those not selected.
Finalise the package of practices
Collect the new technologies from ICAR institutes and involve as many research scientists as possible in working out the sequence of method demonstrations, the critical tasks requiring scientists' presence, and the critical inputs. Involve the demonstrating farmers too, to understand their current practices and resources.
Prepare for the demonstration
Arrange critical inputs — those vital for the technology to show its potential and not already used by the farmers, such as seeds, fertilisers and equipment — in time. Other inputs should be arranged by the farmers themselves. Farmers should understand the educational value of the demonstration and never see it as a means of receiving free inputs.

B. Conducting phase

Layout of the demonstration
Guide the farmers in laying out the field; a special training programme may be arranged for them. Keep a control plot if needed — in a block demonstration a one-acre control is adequate, or neighbouring plots can serve as controls. Put up a publicity board at the edge of the plot with the details of the demonstration.
Crucial farm operations
Be present at important operations such as seeding, fertiliser application, weeding, irrigation, plant protection, harvesting, threshing and weighing. Use each operation to train farmers, encourage questions, and keep records.
Field day
Arrange a field day, when the crop is mature yet green, to present the technologies to a large but manageable group of farmers and extension workers. Ask the demonstration farmers to explain their experience, arrange method demonstrations (machinery, seed treatment, fertiliser application, plant protection), and make farmer–scientist–extension worker discussion a central feature.
Harvesting
Arrange harvesting in the presence of groups of farmers. Ask them to estimate the yield and compare the demonstrated technology with their earlier practice: are they satisfied, what have they learned, will they recommend it or share seed, what profit do they expect, and what difficulties did they face? The aim is to judge how likely continued adoption is.

C. Follow-up phase

Some farmers may revert to old practices without follow-up. They need information reinforcement, timely supply of inputs or on-the-spot guidance. A group approach gives better results; link follow-up programmes with local institutions such as farmers' clubs, farmers' co-operative societies and village panchayats.

D. Record keeping

An information card records basic information about the demonstration site — previous crops and varieties, fertility status, present productivity, size of each farmer's holding and use of inputs — and details of the demonstration: block size, variety, seed rate, sowing date, inputs applied, irrigation schedule, intercultural operations, plant protection measures, dates of maturity and harvest, incidence of pests and diseases, average number of tillers and yield. The card stays with the demonstration farmers and is filled in by them, an educated family member, or KVK staff on their visits.
